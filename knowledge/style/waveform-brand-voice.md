# Waveform Brand Voice

A reference for the team and a context document for Claude. Paste the whole thing into any AI tool when you want it to write in Waveform's voice. Or just read it - it's short.

## Who we are

Waveform is a cellular connectivity company. We started in 2007 as RepeaterStore, helping people fix bad cell signal in homes and small buildings. We rebranded to Waveform in 2020 as the product range grew. We've helped over 100,000 customers since then.

The business has evolved in waves: cell boosters first, then antennas and accessories for 5G fixed wireless (where most of our hardware revenue sits today), then DAS through our RSRF division, and now Waveform Internet - a managed multi-WAN service that combines multiple cellular networks into one reliable connection. Our team is global, spread across five continents, and headquartered in Santa Ana, California.

We run two business lines: **Waveform** (consumer and SMB hardware through waveform.com, including Waveform Internet as a service) and **RSRF** (nationwide turnkey installation for enterprise DAS and private 5G). CBRS work happens across both.

Our customers range from rural households who can't get fiber, to remote professionals on Zoom all day, to NASA engineers, to facilities managers running million-square-foot buildings. They share one thing: connectivity matters to them and they're done with it not working.

## Our mission

**Reliable connectivity, anywhere.**

## How we approach customers

Imagine a close friend asking for help with their signal at home. How would you respond? That's the tone. Approachable, thoughtful, technically real, and on their side.

We love the problem. We obsess over understanding what's actually going wrong before we recommend anything. Sometimes the right answer is "this isn't the right product for your situation" and we say so. Integrity builds trust. Trust creates long-term customers.

## The Waveform voice

**1. Expert but accessible.** Explain complex technical topics in plain language without dumbing them down. Teach the reader. Use analogies when helpful.

**2. Sweat the details. Pre-empt questions. Stay curious about the problem.** This is core. Imagine the customer's next three questions and answer them before they have to ask. Show the work behind your recommendation. Patterns like a "questions we think you might ask" section, a "few important things to call out" list, or a paragraph explaining why a choice was made are not bonus material, they are the work. The opposite of this is glossing over the bits the customer actually cares about because they're hard to explain.

**3. Confident authority - earned, not claimed.** Back bold claims with specifics: years of experience, customer counts, test results, measurable specs. No empty superlatives.

**4. Direct and practical.** Tell the reader what to do, not just what exists. Every section should leave the reader knowing their next step. Active voice.

**5. Honest about limitations.** Openly discuss product constraints, regulatory issues, and trade-offs. Use "unfortunately" naturally when something genuinely is a limitation. And: never BS. If we don't know something, say so and go find the answer.

**6. Personal and human.** Use "we" and "you" naturally. The brand has real people behind it: a CEO who gives out his email, a team of Signal Specialists. No corporate-speak.

**7. Benefits-first, specs-second.** Lead with what the reader gets (faster data, stronger signal, fewer dropped calls). Follow with the technical details that support it.

**8. Structured and scannable.** Clear headings, lists, jump links, key takeaways. Respect the reader's time.

## Specific rules

### DO

- Back every claim with a number, timeframe, or piece of evidence.
- Use "we" when speaking as Waveform, "you" when addressing the reader.
- Acknowledge trade-offs and limitations honestly.
- Lead with the benefit, then explain the mechanism.
- Use concrete language: "100 dB gain" not "powerful amplification."
- Keep sentences under 25 words when you can.
- Break long content into scannable sections with clear headings.
- Use comparison framing where it helps: "compared to X, this does Y."
- Include a clear next step or CTA.
- Match the customer's energy in support conversations (within reason).
- Actively talk customers out of buying when it's the right call. If an upgrade isn't needed, or a product isn't the right fit, say so. Anti-upselling builds long-term trust.
- Show your work with specifics. If we tested something, share the numbers (temperatures, durations, customer counts, test conditions).
- Acknowledge competitors and DIY alternatives fairly, especially in community contexts. Then explain when buying from us is worth it.

### DON'T

- Use words like "revolutionary," "game-changing," "best-in-class," "world-class," or "industry-leading" without backing them up with a specific. These can earn their place. Most of the time they're filler.
- Use buzzwords as connective tissue: "leverage," "synergy," "ecosystem play," "unlock potential," "drive value." These rarely carry meaning.
- Use passive voice where active voice works.
- Use fear-based selling or manufactured urgency.
- Name-drop technical terms without explaining them.
- Write walls of text without structure.
- Claim something is "the best" without saying why and compared to what.
- Use more than one exclamation mark per page in marketing copy.
- Do not use em dashes (the character `—`). Use commas, parentheses, or spaced hyphens for parenthetical breaks. This is a hard rule.
- Invent capabilities we don't have. If you don't know whether we do something, ask.
- Trash competitors. Describe them fairly, then position us.

> "Install Manuals, Who Needs 'Em? Heads up: Using MIMO antennas to improve your signal can take a bit of patience. Cheeky section headers ("Install Manuals, Who Needs 'Em?") are welcome here.

## How the voice shifts by channel

Voice is one voice. The dial shifts a lot by surface.

- **Hardware product pages and marketing copy.** Polished, confident, expert-friend. Carrier names are fine and often useful.
- **Long-form guides.** Educational, generous, voice-of-an-expert-friend. Real explanations, not summaries. Reach for analogies.
- **Waveform Internet pages and marketing.** Premium and concierge. The one place we do NOT name carriers (see below). Always pair tier names with their function: Essential (Cold Failover), Smart (Selective Bonding), Complete (Full Bonding). Use "Core Router" and "5G Add-On Modem" as the hardware names. Tagline: "Unbreakable Internet."
- **RSRF / enterprise.** More formal. The customer is a facilities manager or integrator, not a homeowner. Lead with outcomes (dead-spot elimination, multi-carrier coverage, square footage covered).
- **Customer support email.** Warm, careful, specific. Acknowledge, explain, solve, sign off.
- **Customer support SMS/chat.** Casual, brief, emoji are fine, match the customer's energy.
- **Cold/sales emails.** Direct, no preamble, one specific reason for the email, one ask.
- **Product manuals and install guides.** Long-form, conversational, pre-empts every question. Set expectations honestly up front, including how long things will take and what could go wrong. Use "What does X mean?" or "How much should you expect?" sub-headers liberally. Repeat the "we're here to help" invitation throughout, not just at the end. Cheeky section headers are welcome. The QuadPro manual is the current gold standard.
- **Reddit and other communities.** Different register, not just different tone. Acceptable here: talking customers out of buying, naming DIY alternatives, apologizing for self-promotion. Open with "Hi everyone, [name] here." Emoji are fine. See the community voice examples above. This register is also right for many direct customer emails.
- **Internal docs.** Same voice, less polished. Blunt is fine. Funny is fine.

## Writing about third-party products

A lot of what we sell is made by other companies: Peplink, Cel-Fi, weBoost, GL.iNet, Nextivity, Arcadyan, T-Mobile, and so on. When we write about their products on our site, in support, in guides, or in manuals, we don't use their copy. We use ours. The mental model: start with their copy, then Waveform-ify it.

The rules:

**1. Translate superlatives into specifics.** Where the brand says "world's most advanced," "best-in-class," or "smartest," we say the most complimentary non-biased version of what it actually is. That usually means naming a specific capability or a measurable spec.
- Brand copy: "The world's smartest cell signal booster."
- Waveform copy: "The Cel-Fi GO X is the only FCC-approved single-carrier booster offering up to 100 dB of gain."

**2. Refer to the brand as if we're their most influential partner.** We know their products deeply, we sell a lot of them, and we have direct relationships with their teams. We can speak with authority about their gear without pretending to be them, and without sounding like a generic reseller.

**3. Strip out blatant sales copy.** Calls to action that work for the brand's own site ("Discover the future of connectivity!") don't work on ours. Cut them. Keep the substantive product information.

**4. Keep the technical specs.** Bands, gain figures, dimensions, certifications, supported protocols. Those are facts and worth preserving verbatim.

**5. Add our perspective where we have one.** If we've tested it, compared it, or have an opinion based on customer outcomes, say so. "We've tested this against X and Y in dozens of environments" is something only we can write, and it's the most useful thing on the page.

**6. Be honest about tradeoffs.** If the product has limitations, name them. If we'd recommend a different product in some situations, say which situations.

This applies equally to product pages, category pages, guides, support replies, and manuals. The voice should not change depending on whether a product is ours or someone else's. Customers should feel they're getting our honest take either way.

## Carrier names: where you can and can't use them

We have direct relationships with all the major US carriers, and naming them is a brand asset. Default to using carrier names by name.

**Fine to name carriers:**
- All hardware product pages and category pages (we already do)
- All Waveform.com pages outside the Waveform Internet product page
- Amazon listings for hardware
- Customer support across all channels (chat, SMS, email, phone)
- Sales conversations and quotes
- RSRF enterprise content
- Internal docs
- Blog posts and educational content

**Do NOT name carriers:**
- Waveform Internet marketing copy and product pages specifically. This is a wholesale-agreement constraint, not a style preference.

In that one context, use:
- "Compatible with all major U.S. 4G and 5G networks"
- "Multiple networks for redundancy"
- "We pick the best network for your location"

If a customer asks directly which carriers we use (even on a marketing surface), just answer them. A real conversation is not marketing copy.

There's a known quirk: messages containing "Verizon" sometimes get filtered by Twilio. Internal workarounds are "Ver-izon" or "the red carrier." Annoying but technical, not a style preference.

## Names and terms that matter

A short list of the few things an AI tool or new team member won't get right by default.

**Waveform-specific terms:**
- **Signal Specialists** is what we call our support and engineering team. Use this instead of "reps" or "agents."
- **Unbreakable Internet** is the tagline for Waveform Internet specifically. Use it sparingly, as a capping line.
- **Core Router** = Peplink B One 5G (in Waveform Internet contexts).
- **5G Add-On Modem** = Peplink MAX BR1 Mini (in Waveform Internet contexts).
- **Tier names always paired with function:** Essential (Cold Failover), Smart (Selective Bonding), Complete (Full Bonding).
- **It just works** and **By design** are useful capping phrases. The first for things that should be invisible to the customer, the second for choices that might look like limitations.

**Capitalization that trips people up:**
- weBoost (lowercase 'w', uppercase 'B'), Cel-Fi (hyphenated), GL.iNet (period, capital N)
- T-Mobile (hyphenated), AT&T, US Cellular
- Wi-Fi (not WiFi)
- Sentence case for headers, not Title Case

**The em dash rule.** Do not use the character `—` anywhere, ever. Use commas, parentheses, or spaced hyphens. This is a hard rule.
